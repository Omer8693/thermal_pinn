OverviewAbstract

Water quenching of A356 aluminium castings generates rapidly evolving, non-uniform temperature fields that drive residual stress and shape distortion. Finite Element Method (FEM) simulation resolves these fields accurately, but each solver call is computationally expensive — making repeated design iterations impractical for engineering practice.

This thesis extends the NAS-PINN framework [2] to transient thermal problems through a Multi-Step Window Prediction (MSWP) strategy. The simulation is divided into short time windows; a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) predicts the temperature field within each window, periodically re-anchored by a FEM solve. A skip factor k governs the accuracy–cost trade-off. Three neural architectures, selected by Neural Architecture Search (NAS), are evaluated across ten benchmark geometries spanning 2D, 3D, and a 3D Thermal Fin — achieving up to 85% reduction in FEM solver calls while keeping mean absolute error below the 15°C engineering acceptance threshold.

MethodologyFramework & Method

FEM — Finite Element Method

The FEM solver provides the high-fidelity temperature field at the start of each prediction window. It is computationally expensive but necessary to prevent error accumulation. In this work, FEM uses a Crank–Nicolson time-stepping scheme (Δt = 0.5 s) on unstructured meshes of each benchmark geometry.

PINN — Physics-Informed Neural Network

The PINN is trained to satisfy the heat equation and boundary conditions within each window, starting from the FEM anchor. By embedding physics as a loss term, the network learns to predict the temperature field without requiring labelled training data — only the governing PDE, boundary conditions, and the FEM anchor at window start.

MSWP — Multi-Step Window Prediction

MSWP divides the 30-second quench into consecutive prediction windows. At the start of each window, FEM provides the current temperature field as a fixed anchor. The PINN predicts the field over the remaining steps of the window. After k windows, FEM is called again to re-anchor the prediction. This cycle repeats until the end of the simulation.

Skip Factor k

The skip factor k is the central efficiency parameter. It sets how many consecutive windows are predicted by the PINN before the next FEM solve.

Adaptive-k Controller

Rather than fixing k for the entire simulation, the adaptive-k controller adjusts it window-by-window. After each prediction window, the controller compares the per-window MAE to two thresholds: if error is low it promotes k (skip more FEM calls); if error exceeds the upper threshold it demotes k (call FEM sooner). This allows the system to behave like k=1 during the steep early transient and shift toward k=5 as the field relaxes — automatically and without any manual tuning.

NAS-Selected Architectures

Bayesian / TPE

5 hidden layers × 151 neurons, ReLU activation, Fourier coordinate embedding. Largest capacity (~93K parameters). Best on geometrically irregular domains.

NSGA-II

3 hidden layers × 153 neurons, tanh activation. Compact architecture (~70K parameters). Competitive on smooth geometries at lower training cost.

NSGA-III

3 hidden layers × 75 neurons, tanh activation. Smallest footprint (~17K parameters). Performs similarly to NSGA-II on regular domains.

NAS Transfer (baseline)

Architecture [90, 70, 30, 110] taken directly from Wang & Zhong (2024) without modification. Used as a baseline reference in the 2D adaptive-k study.

Study 1

2D Adaptive-k & NAS Transfer Comparison

Four 2D benchmark domains — Square, Circle, L-Shape, Flower — were used to evaluate the adaptive-k controller and to compare the NAS Transfer architecture from Wang & Zhong (2024) against the three architectures found in this work. The NAS Transfer architecture was taken directly from the reference paper without retraining and evaluated at seed 0 only as a single-run baseline.

On smooth, regular geometries (Square, Circle) all architectures produce nearly identical results. On the L-Shape and Flower — which have reentrant corners and concave boundaries that concentrate temperature gradients locally — Bayesian/TPE achieves significantly lower error than the compact NSGA variants. This demonstrates that geometry-specific NAS adds value only when the target domain contains gradient concentration features absent from the original NAS search domain.

Study 2

2D Canonical Fixed-k Sweep

Three canonical 2D domains — Rectangle, Circle, L-Shape — were used for a systematic fixed-k sweep from k=1 to k=5. All three NAS-found architectures were evaluated at every skip factor with ten independent random seeds each, giving 45 experimental configurations in total. This study characterises the accuracy–efficiency trade-off curve for each geometry.

Bayesian/TPE achieves the lowest error at all k values and across all three domains. On the Rectangle (simplest geometry, uniform boundary normals), all three architectures remain well within the 15°C threshold even at maximum k=5. On the L-Shape, the compact NSGA variants approach or exceed the threshold at k=5, while Bayesian/TPE stays comfortably within it. The ten-seed standard deviation confirms reproducible training with low variance.

Study 3

3D Canonical Fixed-k Sweep

Four 3D benchmark domains — Cylinder, L-Shape, Rectangular, Stacked — extend the fixed-k sweep into three dimensions. Moving from 2D to 3D substantially increases field complexity: boundary conditions act on three-dimensional surfaces and spatial gradients are harder to represent with the same network capacity. All architectures were tested at k=1 through k=5 with ten seeds each.

Bayesian/TPE continues to outperform the NSGA variants across all four 3D domains. All architectures pass the 15°C threshold at k=1. At higher k values, the Stacked domain (most complex 3D geometry) challenges all architectures, while the Cylinder and Rectangular domains remain within the acceptance threshold for Bayesian/TPE even at k=5. The adaptive-k controller, tested alongside fixed-k, achieves near-optimal accuracy automatically.

Study 4

Thermal Fin (3D)

The Thermal Fin is a 3D geometry adapted from the RBniCS steady-state benchmark library and converted to a transient quenching problem. It consists of a central rod with four protruding fins, each with a different thermal conductivity. This benchmark combines interior material interfaces, irregular fin boundaries, and strong local gradient variation — all in three dimensions. It is the most challenging geometry in this study.

Two architectural features proved critical on this benchmark:

  • Fourier embedding — maps spatial coordinates through learned sinusoidal features before the network, enabling accurate representation of the sharp temperature gradients at fin-root junctions. Reduces MAE by 1.8–4.4°C compared to plain coordinate input.
  • Endpoint supervision — forces the PINN to match the FEM solution at the last time step of each window, preventing error drift between consecutive windows. Without it, MAE reaches 58–92°C.

SummaryOverall Results

Key FindingsConclusions

  • ✓ MSWP reduces FEM cost by up to 85% while keeping mean absolute error below the 15°C engineering acceptance threshold across all ten benchmark geometries in 2D, 3D, and the 3D Thermal Fin. The adaptive-k controller achieves this without any manual tuning of the skip factor.
  • ✓ FEM anchoring is not optional. PINN-only rollout without periodic re-anchoring fails on every benchmark with irregular geometry or complex boundary conditions — reaching errors of 60–217°C within a few windows. Periodic FEM anchoring is a structural requirement of the MSWP framework, not a fallback.
  • ✓ The adaptive-k controller matches or exceeds the best fixed-k configuration across all benchmarks. It automatically uses FEM more frequently during the steep early transient (low k) and increases the skip factor as the field relaxes (high k), achieving 83–85% FEM saving in 2D and 75–80% in 3D.
  • ✓ Bayesian/TPE with Fourier embedding achieves the lowest error on geometrically complex domains (L-Shape, Flower, 3D Cylinder, Thermal Fin). On smooth, regular geometries, all three NAS architectures perform comparably — confirming that geometry-specific NAS adds the most value when the target domain contains local gradient concentration features absent from the NAS search domain.
  • ✓ Ten-seed evaluation confirms robustness. The standard deviation of MAE remains below 0.10°C on smooth 2D geometries and below 1.5°C on complex 3D domains. The adaptive-k controller selects identical k-schedules across all ten seeds on domains with regular boundaries, confirming that the schedule reflects the physics of the quench rather than random initialisation.
